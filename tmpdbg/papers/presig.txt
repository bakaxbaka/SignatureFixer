A Note on the Security of ECDSA with Presignatures
or Biased Nonces
Geoffroy Couteau∗ † Nikolaos Makriyannis‡
October 19, 2026
Abstract
Over the years, several ECDSA security experiments have been proposed to enable faster
distributed ECDSA signing. These experiments are not known to reduce to the standard ECDSA
game, and recent work (Groth and Shoup, Eurocrypt 2022) shows that some are vulnerable,
especially when combined with raw signing (signing hash digests directly, rather than pre-hashed
messages) and additive key derivation. Furthermore, several of these variants are implemented
in public libraries and used by companies, sometimes in commercially deployed products, yet
lack sound security foundations.
In this note, we revisit the security landscape of these alternative modes of operation for
ECDSA. As our main result, we show that the security of some of these variants, which are
used in the wild, is equivalent in the generic group and random oracle model to a standard
conjecture from fine-grained complexity on the average-case hardness of the k-SUM problem.
We complement our study by proving lower bounds, conditioned on fine-grained complexity
conjectures, on the running time of generic group model attacks against other standard modes
of operation analyzed in (Groth and Shoup, Eurocrypt 2022). Overall, our results provide a
complete picture of the security landscape of ECDSA variants, and reveal an elegant connection
with (old and new) conjectures from the field of fine-grained complexity.

1 Introduction
ECDSA is one of the most widely deployed signature schemes in existence, underpinning much of the
security infrastructure of the Internet. It is also the dominant signature scheme in the blockchain
ecosystem, securing hundreds of millions of transactions every day—the setting that will be our
focus, since the ECDSA variants we study are, to our knowledge, deployed almost exclusively there.
ECDSA signatures have the form (r, σ), where r is derived from the nonce—essentially a random
point on an elliptic curve—and σ depends on the nonce, the secret key, and the message to be
signed. Concretely, given a secret key x ←Zq and a public key X = gx ∈G over an elliptic curve G
of order q generated by g, an ECDSA signature (r, σ) on a message m is obtained by letting R = gk
be a public nonce, and σ = ( F(m) + x ·r) ·k−1 mod q, where r denotes the first coordinate of the
point R on the curve, and F denotes the ECDSA message digest function (implemented with the
SHA family of hash functions).
Threshold ECDSA is one of the most widely deployed protocols for protecting ECDSA keys and
is at the core of numerous commercial solutions [ Fir25; Coi25; Dkl; Pro25; Blo] enabling various
distributed-wallet applications such as treasury management, Wallet-as-a-Service (WaaS) [ Fir] and
embedded wallets [ Dyn; ABCGJM24], among others. In threshold ECDSA, the key is shared
among multiple parties who jointly run an interactive protocol to produce signatures. Despite its
popularity and widespread use, ECDSA is notoriously hard to evaluate in a distributed setting.
All existing protocols are resource-intensive, incurring high costs in at least one of the following:
computation, communication, or round complexity.
Presignatures and Biased Nonces. To remedy this situation, several works have introduced
alternative modes of operation for ECDSA. These modes of operation fall in two categories:
• Presignatures, introduced independently in [ CMP20; GG20; DOKSS20; DJNPØ20], let the
nonce be precomputed before the message to be signed is available, enabling extremely fast,
succinct, and non-interactive signature generation once the message is given.
• Biased nonces , initially introduced in [ DKLs18] and recently revisited in [ ABCGJM24],
sidestep the need for a commit-and-open phase in threshold ECDSA by letting one party
see its opponent’s share of the nonce before choosing its own, enabling efficient round-optimal
protocols (one offline round, one online round).
Within each category, the modes differ by whether, and how, they integrate a rerandomization
step to attempt to strengthen their security guarantees: early presignature proposals let the par-
ticipants construct a shared nonce R (via Diffie-Hellman agreement) and set the final nonce ˆR to
ˆR = R. Others, such as [ Fir25], rerandomize the nonce multiplicatively as ˆR = Rµ for a random µ
(derived after constructing R via a random oracle query). More recently, Groth and Shoup [ GS22]
suggested using additive rerandomization instead: ˆR = R·gµ. Similarly, known biased-nonce modes
use different rerandomizers: Doerner et al. [ DKLs18] set ˆR = R ·Rδ
1 for random δ, where R1 = gk1 ,
R2 = gk2 , and R = gk1k2 , with k1 held by the first-message sender (initiator) in the protocol and k2
by the second-message sender (responder). The recent work of Adjedj et al. [ ABCGJM24] instead
uses ˆR = R ·Rδ
2. These choices yield different adversarial capabilities and leakage profiles.
Security Concerns for ECDSA Modes of Operation. Despite their efficiency, these modi-
fications are not innocuous: for all presignature and biased-nonce variants, security is not known
3
to reduce to the standard ECDSA notion. Indeed, there is good reason for this: Groth and Shoup
[GS22] showed that ECDSA with presignatures requires, at a minimum, an additional assumption
on the hash function 1. This implies that each variant requires a dedicated security analysis to be
confidently relied upon.
The situation is further complicated by the fact that presignature modes of operation are also
often considered in conjunction with other (orthogonal) modifications of the ECDSA security game:
• In raw signing , the message digest—rather than the message itself—is passed as input to
the signatories. Raw signing is useful to many providers, as it enables them to offer signing
capabilities for variants of ECDSA with custom hash functions. It is also useful when end
users wish to hide the actual contents of their messages from the provider. It is worth noting
that raw signing is typically offered in HSMs [ Pkc].
• With additive key derivation , many subkeys Xγ = X ·gγ can be derived from a single master
key X. This mechanism is used widely in cryptocurrency applications due to the Hierarchi-
cal Deterministic Wallet scheme standardized in Bitcoin Improvement Proposal 32 (BIP32,
[Wui12]).
Using presignatures (without rerandomization) in combination with these variants introduces
other attack vectors: presignatures are utterly insecure in the raw-signing mode of operation, and
admit faster generic attacks when they are combined with additive key derivation [ GS22].
A first systematic study of the ECDSA landscape was initiated by Groth and Shoup [ GS22],
showing attacks on presignatures with either raw-signing or key-derivation, and proving the security
in the elliptic-curve generic group model (EC-GGM, a variant of the generic group model (GGM)
introduced in [ Sho97] that models more closely the setting of elliptic curve groups) of additively-
rerandomized presignatures (even in conjunction with raw-signing or key-derivation). In the biased-
nonce setting, the scheme of Adjedj et al. [ ABCGJM24] comes with a tight security proof in the
EC-GGM. However, there remain multiple gaps in our understanding of the security landscape of
these ECDSA variants:
• For presignatures without rerandomization, with or without additive key-derivation, it is not
clear to what extent the cryptanalysis of [ GS22] in the EC-GGM translates to a real-world
attack, as the attack model counts only the number of queries to the generic group, but
ignores the cost of all other operations. Furthermore, it is not known whether these attacks
are best-possible, or whether better attacks could exist.
• For presignatures with multiplicative rerandomization [ CMP20], as used in [ Fir25] (and by at
least one other company [ com25]), no supporting security analysis is known in the literature.
• Finally, the biased nonce mode of Doerner et al. [ DKLs18] (deprecated, but implemented in
several public libraries [ Tau21; Coi21; Qui23b] and used by some companies [ Qui23a]) claims
security in the GGM, but only a sketch of the analysis is provided. As we will see later,
the situation for this mode is more complex than originally anticipated by Doerner et al.
[DKLs18].

As threshold ECDSA and its many modes of operation are routinely used to secure monetary
transactions, the lack of a clear security landscape is a concern. Our aim in this work is to provide
a complete picture of the security landscape. Along the way, we reveal an elegant connection of
this landscape with the field of fine-grained complexity theory.
Important update: a new 3SUM algorithm
Alman and Vassilevska Williams [ A V26] report a truly subquadratic algorithm for 3SUM,
discovered by an LLM and subsequently checked and developed by the authors. Assuming
its correctness, this refutes our 3-SUM hardness assumptions and the associated conjectured
security bounds for multiplicatively rerandomized presignatures and the DKLS18 biased-nonce
variant [DKLs18]. The reductions establishing a connection in both directions between forgery
and average-case computational problems remain valid. Given how recent this development
is, we retain the original statements subject to this qualification and defer a fuller revision
pending further scrutiny.
1.1 Our Contribution
In this work, we aim to fill the remaining gaps and provide a formal and unified security analysis
of all known presignatures and biased-nonce modes of operation for ECDSA. Our study reveals a
close connection between the hardness of ECDSA modes of operation in the elliptic-curve generic
group model and random oracle, and standard conjectures in the field of fine-grained complexity
regarding the average-case hardness of the k-SUM problem. Before stating our main result, let us
introduce a few notions. The average-case k-SUM conjecture at density 1 states the following: let
L be a list of N1/k random integers between −N and N. Then no algorithm can find k integers
a1, ··· , ak ∈L such that a1 + ··· + ak = 0 in time much faster than N⌈k/2⌉/k. The standard form
of the conjecture rules out algorithms with constant success probability. A slightly stronger form
of the conjecture, due to [ DKK22], rules out algorithms with slightly smaller success probability
Ω(1/ log2 N). Let F : {0, 1}∗ →Zq denote the ECDSA message digest function. We prove the
following:
Theorem 1.1 (informal). We model F as a random oracle and let ε denote an arbitrary fixed
constant. Then in the EC-GGM, there exists an attacker running in time T = q1/2−ε with suc-
cess probability Ω(1) against the biased-nonce mode of operation from [ DKLs18], or against the
presignature mode of operation with multiplicative randomizer from [ Fir25], if and only if the
strong average-case 3-SUM conjecture at density 1 is wrong. Furthermore, if 3-SUM at density 1
can be solved in near-optimal time ˜O(N1/3), these two modes of operation can be broken in time
T = ˜O(q1/3) in the EC-GGM.
The hardness of the average-case 3-SUM problem at density 1 is a standard and well-studied
hardness assumption in the field of fine-grained complexity. A statement of the conjecture can be
found for example in [ DKK22, Conjecture 1.2], or in [ ASSVV24, Conjecture 1.1] (the conjecture is
stated there for a modular variant of k-SUM, but the variants are equivalent). Other statements of
the same conjecture can be found in [ Set15; LL W19; BSDV21; AHY25; DLSVW25]. The stronger
form is due to [ DKK22], where it was noted that even a refutation of this stronger variant would
imply a major breakthrough, since the slightly sub-constant winning probability would suffice in
5
known cryptanalytic applications of k-SUM algorithms. This implies that finding any attack that
breaks these modes of operation faster than the best known attacks on standard ECDSA would
entail a major breakthrough in fine-grained complexity.
We note that a byproduct of our analysis is a proof that these two modes of operation fall short
of the q1/2 security bar in a “pure-GGM” model, where the adversary runtime is counted solely
via GGM queries: both modes admit a forgery using only q1/3 queries to the GGM (in contrast,
the best known attack on plain ECDSA requires solving a discrete logarithm and takes time q1/2).
This contradicts a claim of Doerner et al. [ DKLs18] that their alternative mode admits “a proof
in the generic group model that the adversary has no advantage in the forgery game, relative to
the standard functionality” . This could be a concern, since this protocol is still maintained in
some public libraries [ Qui23b] and used by some companies [ Qui23a], even though the authors
of [ DKLs18] deprecated their protocol (for reasons that include other unrelated security concerns,
and because it was superseded by the protocol of Doerner et al. [ DKLs24]). This is also the
case for the protocol of [ Fir25], which is actually deployed commercially. Fortunately, barring a
breakthrough in fine-grained complexity, our analysis shows that this pure-GGM attacker cannot
be turned into a real-world attack 2.
We complement our analysis by investigating the original “plain” presignature mode of operation
(without rerandomizer), which was previously shown in [ GS22] to be broken in the EC-GGM using
q1/3 queries, or even q1/4 queries when the mode is combined with additive key-derivation. We
observe that the attacks from [ GS22] are pure-GGM attacks (that ignore all attacker costs beyond
group queries), and we ask whether they can be turned into real-world attacks. We prove the
following:
Theorem 1.2 (informal). There exists a q1/3-time attack against the plain presignature mode of
operation, with or without additive key-derivation, that uses the group generically. Furthermore,
assuming that F is modeled as a random oracle and the strong average-case 4-SUM conjecture at
density 1, there exist no q1/3−ε-time attacks on this mode without additive key-derivation. Assuming
additionally a new (modular) affine collision ( AFF) conjecture, there exist no q1/3−ε-time attacks
even with additive key-derivation.
The new AFF conjecture states, informally, that given two lists L1, L2 of random elements of Zq,
no q1/3−ε-time adversary can find two lines (a, b) ∈L1 ×L2, (c, d) ∈L1 ×L2, and a point x ∈L2,
such that both lines coincide on x: ax + b = cx + d mod q (see Security Game 4 and Conjecture 5.1
for a formal statement). One way to attack AFF is by reduction to a 4-SUM instance: pick an
arbitrary x ∈L2, and define L3 = {u ·x : u ∈L1}. Then, a solution to AFF is given by a 4-tuple
(u, v, w, y ) ∈L3 ×L2 ×L3 ×L2 such that u + v −w −y = 0 mod q, which can be obtained in time
q1/3 (ignoring other polynomial factors) using Wagner’s 4-list algorithm [ Wag02] or some optimized
variants over Zq [JKL24]. Our conjecture essentially states that this is the best possible attack.
We note that the attack (and lower bound) matches that of [ GS22] in the setting without
key-derivation. In the setting with key-derivation, our results indicate that the plain presignature
mode is plausibly more secure than the pure-GGM attack of Groth and Shoup [ GS22] suggests, if
a new average-case fine-grained hardness assumption holds; we leave the study of this conjecture
for future work. We summarize all known results on the security of ECDSA modes of operation,

an adversary that chooses the message to be signed adaptively, after seeing the presignature and,

strongly influence them. Second, the key-derivation attacks require the number d of available

not broken in any practical sense—it offers less security than standard ECDSA, but the resulting
security level may still be adequate. We reserve the word “insecure” for modes that admit a forgery

1.2 Related Work: Rerandomized Presignatures
As highlighted in Table 1 and detailed in Section 2.3, plain (non-rerandomized) presignatures incur
a meaningful security loss relative to standard ECDSA, and they are utterly insecure in raw-signing
mode.
In an effort to recover the security level of ECDSA while retaining raw signing, Groth and Shoup
[GS22] introduce rerandomized presignatures: the presignature R is rerandomized to a modified
point ˆR, and the released signature uses nonce ˆR instead of R. Groth and Shoup [ GS22] propose
additive rerandomization, setting ˆR = R ·gδ for a public random δ. They show that if a single
modifier δ is sampled and bound to one message digest, so only one modifier can be used and the
presignature is discarded otherwise, then additive rerandomized presignatures match the security
of standard ECDSA. However, this mitigation must be implemented with care: in Groth and Shoup
[GS22], once the adversary chooses a message and presignature, the signature is generated and the
presignature is consumed. This setting is only realistic when a public random beacon is available.
In contrast, using the standard practice of performing rerandomization via a hash function (hashing
the transcript à la Fiat-Shamir) would be insecure, since it allows the adversary to “backtrack”
and reject a rerandomization outcome that is not to their liking. Unfortunately, in many scenarios,
a public trusted random beacon is too strong a setup assumption.
As a consequence, several threshold ECDSA libraries [ Fir25] implement a variant of reran-
domized presignatures that differs from what is prescribed in Groth and Shoup [ GS22], and the
limited security guarantees in that work do not extend to these variants. Additionally, while Groth
and Shoup [ GS22] prescribe that the nonce be randomized using an additive public shift, multiple
implementations instead apply a multiplicative factor.
In the recent work [ ABCGJM24] on biased nonces (biased nonces and presignatures are essen-
tially equivalent as we explain in Section 2.2), the authors show that even when an attacker can
sample many different additive modifiers for different message digests (and even multiple modifiers
for the same digest), with each modifier the output of a hash modeled as a random oracle, there
is essentially no security loss relative to standard ECDSA. However, the analysis of Adjedj et al.
[ABCGJM24] only applies to additive modifiers, and, as far as we know, no such analysis exists for
multiplicative modifiers or biased nonces à la [ DKLs18].
1.3 Organization
In Section 2, we introduce preliminaries on the generic group model, presignatures (including an
equivalence to biased nonces and a discussion and overview of known attacks), and the k-SUM
problem. Section 3 presents a new attack on rerandomized presignatures. Section 4 introduces our
main reduction from the security of our general presignature experiment to a multiplicative variant
of the k-SUM problem, while Section 5 extends the reduction to the setting of plain presignatures
(without rerandomization), under stronger fine-grained complexity conjectures. Finally, Section 6
8
shows that the conjectured hardness of the multiplicative variants of k-SUM used in our work is
implied by the standard “average-case k-SUM at density 1” conjecture.

2.2.1 Mapping Biased-Nonce ECDSA to Security Game 1.
While not immediate, Security Game 1 also captures the biased-nonce setting in threshold ECDSA.
In this regime, the initiator sends R1 = gk1 ; the responder samples R2 = gk2 and finalizes the nonce
as a deterministic function of its view (the function includes a cryptographic hash for security).
Below we show that, modeling the hash as a random oracle, the security of biased nonces is captured
by Security Game 1. We stress that the biased-nonce effect does not affect an honest responder
(the nonce is unbiased from their perspective), so the analysis considers only a corrupted responder
with an honest initiator.

2.4 The k-SUM Problem
Definition 2.3 (A verage-casek-SUM problem) . An input to the (N, r)-kSUM problem is given by
a list L of r integers sampled uniformly and independently over {−N, ··· , N}. The goal is to find
a k-tuple (an ordered set of distinct indices) (i1, ··· , ik) such that L[i1] + ···+ L[ik] = 0 , where the
sum is over Z.
Instances of the average-case k-SUM problem are usually characterized by their density regime.

(where the approximation is very close to reality for low values of k; in this work, we will always

the dense regime ∆ > 1, many solutions exist, while in the sparse regime ∆ < 1, solutions are
unlikely to exist. A folklore and well-studied conjecture states that k-SUM is average-case hard at
density 1:
Conjecture 2.4 (A verage-casek-SUM conjecture). Any algorithm that solves the (N, N 1/k)-kSUM
problem with probability Ω(1) must run in expected time T = Ω( N⌈k/2⌉/k−o(1)).
A statement of the above conjecture can be found for example in [ DKK22, Conjecture 1.2], or
in [ ASSVV24, Conjecture 1.1] (the conjecture is stated there for a modular variant of kSUM, but
as we will see later, the variants are equivalent). Other statements of the same conjecture (or
generalizations thereof) can be found in [ Set15; LL W19; BSDV21; AHY25; DLSVW25]. In our
work, we will rely on a slight strengthening of this conjecture:
Conjecture 2.5 (A verage-casek-SUM conjecture at ε = 1/ log2 N). Any algorithm that solves
the (N, N 1/k)-kSUM problem with probability Ω(1/ log2 N) must run in expected time T =
